Chapter 6 – The Interrogation
Date: December 15, 2888
Time: 2330 Galactic Standard Time (GTC)
Location: GFC Base: Gladius

In the dimly lit interrogation chamber, The Surgeon stood over the captive in complete stillness. As an amplified Mindreaver, he had no need for the racks of instruments lesser interrogators relied on. His gift was attention — a depth and precision of mind-reading that GRID had spent his whole life sharpening down to a single edge. Where another man would cut and burn for hours and come away with fragments, The Surgeon simply went inside.
His method was something few outside GRID had ever seen, and fewer still understood. He did not pry information out of a mind; he could only read what the mind agreed to show him. A memory surrendered willingly surfaced clear and whole — and from a single glimpsed image he could reckon the rest in an instant: numbers, positions, the shape of a formation, the distance between two points. A memory withheld stayed obscured — fog, or static, or a blank where a thing should have been. So his real craft was twofold. First, to bring a subject to the point of willingness, because a mind could be made willing if you wore its resistance down far enough. Second, and far harder, to know with certainty whether what surfaced was the truth or only its shape: a clean memory, or a clever fog dressed up to look like one. The amplification helped with the first. The rest was simply him, paying closer attention than anyone alive.
The wearing was done with time. With a bare hand laid against a subject's skull he could fold time as the mind experienced it, stretching a few real seconds into what felt, to the captive, like hours — hours of being walked back through its own life, leaned on, asked the same thing a hundred different ways, until holding the fog in place cost more than letting it lift. No marks. No blood. Nothing the body would carry afterward. Only the mind would ever know what had been done to it.
He began, as he always did, by stripping the subject of its armor and helmet — not for cruelty, but for access. Each piece came away methodically, until the creature beneath was laid bare.
As the armor came off, the alien's true form was exposed. It was a stark contrast to the imposing figure it had presented in its battle attire. The alien's skin was a mottled grey, smooth and seemingly seamless, almost like a layer of natural armor. Its body was slender yet muscular, built for agility. The head was slightly elongated, with no hair, and its face was a tapestry of unique features: large, dull grey eyes, a slit-like mouth with sharp teeth, and a narrow-tipped nose. The ears were mere indentations on the side of its head. Its fingers were long and ended in what appeared to be delicate, yet potentially lethal hand-like claws.
The Surgeon studied the exposed alien the way he studied everything — completely, and without feeling. He laid a bare hand against the side of its skull.
"You are going to give me everything you have," he said quietly. It was not a threat. It was a schedule. "It will be easier if you don't fight. It will not matter if you do."
Then he went still, and the chamber went quiet, and inside the alien's mind the hours began.
Images surfaced — the unguarded ones first, the things a mind shows before it thinks to hide them — and The Surgeon read them as they came: distant worlds, star charts, military formations; a harsh, unforgiving homeworld; brutal training regimens; a culture that glorified the thrill of battle and the glory of dying against a worthy adversary. He did not need it explained. From a single glimpse of a fleet at anchor he could count the hulls and read their order. Each fragment was a piece of the puzzle; he turned each one over, set it in its place, and reached for the next, patient as a man sorting files.
The alien lived through something else entirely. To anyone watching through the glass it only sat rigid in the chair, jaw clenched, a sheen of sweat on its grey skin, its mouth opening now and then in a soundless cry. Whatever it was enduring, it endured alone, somewhere no one else could follow.
Captain Orion entered the chamber partway through, his expression caught between concern and unease. It was not brutality that unsettled him — there was none to see. It was the stillness: a man with one hand on an enemy's skull, both of them silent, and the enemy coming apart anyway. "Surgeon," he began, but The Surgeon answered without looking up.
"GRID has been tracking these creatures for months, Captain," he said. "Star outpost after star outpost, gone without a trace. This is the first one that can tell us why. I don't intend to waste it."
Orion's gaze moved between The Surgeon and the rigid, sweating captive. The silence of the thing conflicted with his sense of honor in a way open cruelty never quite had — but he knew the stakes. The enemy had been a shadow, elusive and deadly. Now, for the first time, that shadow had a mouth, and The Surgeon had his hand inside it.
The Surgeon worked on, unhurried. There was no theater to it, no raised voice, no instruments — only the slow, methodical pressure of a far stronger mind bearing down on a weaker one. Layer by layer, the alien's defenses gave.
What surfaced next was the most important thing yet. This was no mere scout. It was part of an elite military outfit — the Dra'Kor Vhel'Tan — an advance unit, highly trained, dispatched ahead of the main force.
The Surgeon went deeper, and more came loose. The Dra'Kor Vhel'Tan, along with several other teams, had been sent in early to disable Trident's automated planetary defenses and clear the way for the invasion. They had already done it. Their work was finished; most had already left the planet. The invasion was not merely impending — it had been meticulously planned and was already in motion.
And beneath the operational detail lay the motive. The Obsidians, as the alien thought of its own kind, were driven by a sacred duty: they had come for an artifact that had been activated on Trident, and its activation was, to them, a desecration — a sacrilege that demanded retribution. Their plan was simple and absolute. Retrieve the relic. Punish humanity for the heresy of having touched it.
Captain Orion, who had been observing the interrogation, was struck by the gravity of this information. The alien's identity as a member of the Dra'Kor Vhel'Tan and the revelation of the Obsidians' sacred mission changed the entire scope of the conflict. 
Orion had remembered hearing the words “Dra’Kor Vhel’Tan” over random comms channels before he recalled Fireteam Valor.
 An elite force had already compromised Trident's defenses, and a coalition of alien races was on the brink of launching a full-scale invasion.
The interrogation session had taken a toll on the alien. The once formidable Dra'Kor Vhel'Tan operative now appeared broken and defeated, its body bearing the non-physical scars of The Surgeon's methods. The Surgeon, recognizing the extent of the information extracted, signaled for a pause. The creature was carefully unstrapped and taken away for medical attention, its value as an intelligence source exhausted for now.
Orion and The Surgeon, now alone in the chamber, processed the implications of what they had learned. "We must prepare immediately," Orion said with urgency. "The Obsidian invasion is not just a threat; it's already underway. We need to warn our forces and fortify our manual defenses, especially with our automated systems down. "
As they left the interrogation chamber, the weight of their newfound knowledge was palpable. An elite alien force had infiltrated Trident, and the main Obsidian invasion force was on its way, driven by a deep-seated vendetta against humanity. The race to defend the planet and uncover the secrets of the sacred artifacts was now more critical than ever.
In the war room, members of Fireteam Valor were gathered, the tension palpable as they awaited the debrief. They were a mix of wariness and anticipation, each processing the gravity of their situation in their own way.
Staff Sergeant Rourke leaned against the wall; his arms crossed. "Bet The Surgeon's having the time of his life in there," he said with a grim chuckle. "Wonder if our alien friend is singing yet."
Specialist Harlan snorted. "Singing? More like screaming his damn head off. If those walls could talk."
Lieutenant Argus, sitting at the head of the table, his gaze fixed on the door, replied, "Whatever it takes. We need that intel if we're going to stand a chance against these bastards."
Corporal Vega, her expression hard, added, "I just hope we get something useful out of this mess. We’re sitting ducks here until we know what we’re up against."
Royce shifted uncomfortably in his seat. "This whole thing is screwed up. You saw how much it took to take down those pricks back at Charr.”
Harlan laughed darkly. "Relax, this is our show now, kid. I’m sure we can figure out how to kill them a bit faster."
Throughout the conversation, Wraith remained silent, his demeanor calm and unreadable. He observed his teammates, his expression betraying none of the emotion that seemed to ripple through the others.
Rourke glanced over at Wraith. "What about you, Wraith? You've been quiet. Got any insights with that GRID brain of yours?"
Wraith's gaze shifted from one team member to another. "Emotions won't help us now," he finally said. "Focus on the task at hand. What matters is how we respond to what we learn. The Surgeon will get the information we need; our job is to be ready to take action."
The room fell silent for a moment, each member processing Wraith's words. The reality was stark – they were on the brink of an unprecedented conflict, and the fate of Trident hung in the balance.
As the team continued to wait, their conversation interspersed with moments of silence, the door to the war room finally opened. Captain Orion stepped in; his face etched with the weight of the situation.
"Alright, listen up," Orion began, his voice firm. "We've got our intel. It's time to understand exactly what we're dealing with."
The team straightened up, their attention now fully on Orion. 
Captain Orion stood before Fireteam Valor, his posture embodying the severity of the situation unfolding on Trident. The members of Fireteam Valor, clad in their Praetorian armor, directed their full attention towards him as he began to speak.
"Fireteam Valor, we're at Combat Alert Red. An invasion is already underway," Orion started, his voice steady but laden with urgency. "We have scarce information about the enemy's numbers or the exact timing of their attack. However, we must prepare for the main invasion force as soon as humanly possible."
He paused briefly, ensuring his next words were clearly understood. "Under OE-Exodus-TR Protocol, we're initiating a full-scale evacuation of all civilians to emergency spaceports at Crete City and New Eridanus. There are undisclosed underground bunkers in New Eridanus and reinforced underground tunnels under Crete City that lead to the beachheads. These will hopefully provide a safe haven secondary fall-back point, in the event lift-off is not an option."
Orion's gaze swept across the room. "This evacuation will be executed methodically, with priority given to the most vulnerable - those with medical needs, families with young children, essential personnel, followed by the general populace. It's crucial that this operation is carried out efficiently and without panic."
He continued, detailing the military involvement in the evacuation. "The 82nd OGDF Battalion's Alpha, Bravo, and Delta Companies are tasked with escorting the civilians. They will ensure safe passage and coordinate with local authorities to secure the population.”
The gravity of their defensive position became apparent as Orion shared more troubling news. "Our automated planetary defenses and long-range communication systems are down. We’re running nearly blind and defenseless. But we’re working to manually bring our orbital defense cannons back online. Given the circumstances, it's likely the enemy knows we're aware of their plans. We must be ready to defend this planet, at all costs.”
He shifted his focus to the immediate readiness of Fireteam Valor. "Honor Team is currently stationed in orbit on the Liberation, alongside Commander Drexel. Should you require backup or additional support, STRIKE Team drop shuttles from the Liberation can reach your location within five minutes of the alert."
Orion then provided an update on the incoming support. "Luckily we were just within range of Admiral Greenwich and his minor fleet. He notified STARCOM Fleet of the situation and is en route with twenty ships. Their arrival time is uncertain, but we are hopeful they'll join us for the defense," he paused, looking up at the view screen, “Unfortunately, the rest of STARCOM Fleet is unable to join us in the defense for at least two weeks. We’re on our own.”
Concluding his briefing, Orion emphasized the need for thorough preparedness. "Ensure you're fully equipped and ready for prolonged engagement. Stock up on ammunition, water, and food supplies. Once you deploy, we cannot predict the duration of your mission. Stay vigilant and prepared for any scenario."
“I will be staying behind, Orion. I need to speak with The Surgeon and coordinate a few tasks, once the interrogation is concluded,” said Wraith.
“Do what you need to do, Wraith. Just keep me updated on whatever the hell you’re planning,” Captain Orion replied.
With Orion's briefing concluded, the members of Fireteam Valor began their preparations, each acutely aware of the critical role they were about to play in the defense of Trident and the safety of its civilians. The atmosphere in the war room was one of focused determination, as they braced for the challenges that lay ahead.
Captain Orion returned to the podium, his gaze sweeping across the room filled with Fireteam Valor members, each one ready for the daunting task ahead. His next words were a stark reminder of the grim reality they faced.
"Remember what I said before, men. Kill them all."
In response, the team stood up in unison, embodying the spirit of unity and readiness. Each member took their right hand, made a fist, and pounded it against their left breastplate, the sound resonating through the room. 
Together, they shouted, "STRIKE!" This was more than just a motto; it was a declaration of their resolve and readiness to use deadly force when necessary. It was a call to action, only used in moments where motivation was paramount, and the prospect of combat was imminent.
Orion, bolstered by their display of solidarity, added, "Praetorians fight to the last, men!"
Before he could continue, the moment was interrupted. The Surgeon stepped into the doorway. He did not raise his voice; he never did. "There's more in it than I've taken," he said. "It buried the timing of the invasion deeper than the rest — deep enough that hiding it cost it something. I want it back under before whatever's left of its discipline knits over the gap."
The room's attention sharpened. The Surgeon's reach into a captive mind was an asset too rare to question, and time was the one thing they did not have.
Orion nodded. "Do what you must, Surgeon. We need that intel. Every piece of it could save lives."
As the Surgeon moved to carry out his task, the members of Fireteam Valor prepared to support him in any way necessary. The situation was dire, and every second counted. The team knew the Surgeon's interrogation of the Obsidian could unveil critical details about the impending invasion, potentially giving them an edge in the looming conflict. Fireteam Valor was ready to do whatever it took to protect Trident and its inhabitants, especially Lieutenant Argus, with his family living in New Eridanus.
Captain Orion, with a decisive nod to Lieutenant Argus, issued his command, "Valor Six, you know what to do. Get it done."
Lieutenant Argus, his expression resolute and determined, replied sharply, "Yes, sir!"
Together, Orion and The Surgeon made their way down to the medical room. The corridors of the base were dimly lit, casting long shadows that seemed to stretch endlessly. The hum of the base's machinery and distant sounds of preparation for the impending conflict created an undercurrent of tension as they walked. The air was thick with the sense of impending action, each step taking them closer to a crucial and dark part of their mission.
Upon entering the medical room, they found the captured Obsidian where it had been left. It lay still, mentally spent, its breathing slow and labored — physically whole, unmarked by anything The Surgeon had done, and yet somehow diminished, as though something load-bearing had been quietly drawn out of it.
The Surgeon approached without ceremony. He did not speak to it this time; there was nothing left to say that would change what came next. He drew a stool to the side of the bed, sat, and laid his hand once more against the side of the creature's skull, as unhurried as a physician taking a pulse.
What he was about to do had no name in any GFC manual. It was simply the thing GRID had built him to do — pointed, now, at the one part of this mind that had cost the alien the last of its strength to keep shut.
The room seemed to cool as he reached in. Orion stood a few steps back and watched, and found, as he always did with this man, that the watching was the hard part. There was no struggle to witness, no cruelty to flinch from — only a quiet man with his eyes half-closed and an enemy slowly coming undone in front of him, and the growing certainty that whatever was happening behind that grey brow was worse than anything a blade could have managed. Orion understood the necessity. It did not make the room any warmer.
Inside, The Surgeon went looking for the thing the alien had spent itself to bury. He could not force the door — no Mindreaver could; a mind only ever opened what it chose to. So he waited at it instead, patient across what passed, for the captive, as hours.
And somewhere in the waiting, a memory came loose that had nothing to do with fleets.
The alien had been small once — smaller than its armor would ever suggest, and frightened in a way it had not let itself be frightened since. The Surgeon watched the memory surface unbidden: a low grey settlement beneath three suns, a procession, a child's hand drawn gently — not torn — from its mother's. The creature had not been stolen. It had been given. Among its people, when the armada called for the season's young, you surrendered what was asked and you called it honor, and the ones who wept over it wept quietly. They had fed him into the War of the Five Suns as a boy, because his kind were the sort the armada spent and replaced; and he had proved so much better at surviving it than the rest that they had lifted him out of the fodder and made him Dra'Kor Vhel'Tan. None of it had ever been a thing he chose. The first of it he had been too young to even understand.
The Surgeon held still inside that memory longer than the work required.
He knew the shape of it. Not the suns, not the procession — his own had come in the back of an unmarked transport, a stolen thing, no ceremony and no one bothering to call it honor. But the room at the end of both doors was the same room: a child handed to a machine, and walked back out a weapon. He had been small once, too. He had been afraid, too. No one had asked him, either.
For the length of a held breath, he showed it something back.
Not a word, and not a mercy — and not, either, anything the creature could ever use. He was perhaps the one man alive who could open his own memory the way he opened another's, and choose exactly how much of it would carry across: a boy; a transport with no markings; hands that did not ask; the particular cold of a corridor with a number stitched where a name should have been. The where of it, the who, the long grey years of method that came after — those he kept behind the same fog he had spent his life learning to see through. He gave the alien only the shape that mattered and kept the rest welded shut, and the alien understood it completely, precisely because there was nothing in it left to defend against. *They took me the same way. Yours called it duty and mine called it nothing at all, and look what the two of us became.*
The alien's resistance did not so much break as set down — the way a man sets down a weight he has carried so long he has forgotten it was ever a choice.
After that, it showed him what it had left to show.
The picture came apart in his hands, and he catalogued each piece as it surfaced, calm and exact. An invasion fleet, and its shape: seventy warships. Ten transport ships, each able to carry over a hundred thousand Obsidian soldiers. One major flagship and one minor — command, almost certainly — eighty-two vessels in all. Behind them, more than ten thousand fighter-craft built for air superiority and ground support. It was not merely a large force; it was a thorough one, equipped for a full-scale assault and confident of it.
Only one thing stayed beyond his reach. Where the exact hour of the assault should have been there was only fog — and he knew fog when he saw it. The alien had buried the timing beneath its own ability to recall it on command, obscured it so thoroughly that even willing, it could not have surfaced the memory clean. Either a remarkable strength of will, or a loyalty drilled in past the point where breaking it was possible; it made no difference which. He could see plainly that the thing was being hidden. He simply could not make the creature willing enough, fast enough, to clear it. The Surgeon noted that, too, with the same flat attention he gave everything else — he did not waste effort prying at a door welded shut from the far side. He took what was there, and let the rest be.
When there was nothing left worth the taking, he opened his eyes and lifted his hand away. The alien sagged against the restraints — alive, breathing, present, and entirely emptied of what it had carried onto Trident.
Whether what had passed between them in that compressed dark had been pity, or only the most efficient thing he had ever done, The Surgeon could not have said — and did not try to. For him, the two had stopped being separate questions a long time ago.
"It's done," The Surgeon said. He stood and smoothed the cuff of his sleeve. "Everything it knew that's worth knowing, I have. You'll have it written up within the hour — numbers, formations, intent. Everything but the clock."
Orion looked at the living, hollowed thing in the chair. "And the prisoner?"
"Stays alive." The Surgeon was already turning for the door. "My orders are to deliver this one to GRIDCOM, intact. Don't ask me why — I wasn't told, and I didn't ask." Something almost like dry humor passed behind his eyes and was gone. "We're each only ever shown the room we're standing in, Captain. You know that better than most. Have it sedated and prepped for transport. It won't give your people any trouble. There isn't enough of it left to try."
And then he was gone — as quietly as he did everything. A moment later Orion realized he could not have said precisely when the man had stepped out.
Orion turned his attention to the numbers instead, because the numbers he could do something about: an invasion fleet of eighty-two ships and ten thousand fighters, an elite vanguard already loose and gone, and a timetable he didn't have and now never would. "Report all of it to GRID command," he said — to the room, to himself. "We need to prepare for what's coming." The battle for Trident was approaching, and time was running out.
