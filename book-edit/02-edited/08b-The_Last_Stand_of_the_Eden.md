Chapter 9: The Last Stand of the Eden
Date: December 16, 2888
Time: 0730 Galactic Standard Time (GTC)
Location: Flagship – GFC Eden, high orbit over Trident

*(Provisional number — runs concurrent with the Liberation boarding; see DISCREPANCY-REPORT A3/G13 for renumbering.)*

The light of the Vindicator's death was still fading from the viewport when Admiral Greenwich set his jaw and turned back to the fight.

He had watched Harris ram a dying warship into an enemy flagship, and he had watched the Obsidians answer it with a single beam that swallowed three GFC hulls whole. His oldest friend, gone in a flash that didn't even leave wreckage worth the name. Greenwich rolled the cold cigar from one corner of his mouth to the other and did not let his face move. The bridge was watching him. The bridge was always watching him.

"Set condition one through the fleet," he'd said, and the Eden had answered like a thing alive.

Because it was, very nearly. The Eden did not fly the way other warships flew. On any other vessel in the GFC the pilots sat sealed away in a Neural Bridge two decks down, their commands relayed up through fiber and trust. Here they sat on the main bridge itself, a half-ring of reclined cradles behind the Admiral's station, and the men and women in them did not touch a single control. They were Mindreavers — the rarest the Coalition had, GRID-vetted and fleet-bonded — and the Eden was less a ship they operated than a body they wore. Thought became thrust. Intention became fire. When the lead among them, a quiet woman the crew only ever called Halcyon, drew breath, four hundred meters of warship leaned with her.

"Admiral." Halcyon's voice came without her opening her eyes. It always unsettled the new crew, that the pilots spoke from somewhere far behind their own faces. "The line ships are with us. All of them. I have the whole fleet."

She meant it precisely. Nineteen ships had come out of gravspace with the Eden, and Halcyon and her fellow pilots held every one of them in the same shared, humming awareness — nineteen captains' intentions folded into a single battlefield picture, threat and angle and firing solution passed mind to mind faster than any comms array could carry it. It was the one edge the Coalition had that the enemy did not seem to know existed. Greenwich intended to spend it well, because he intended to spend everything.

"Then let's make them regret coming," he said. "All ships, advance. Titan Cannons free. Halcyon — you have the fleet. Fight it like one fist."

What followed, for eleven minutes, was the finest gunnery Admiral Greenwich had ever seen.

The Obsidian armada was built to crush ships that flew like ships — predictable, lagged, a half-second of human reaction stitched into every turn. The Eden's flotilla did not have that half-second. Where an enemy gunner led a target, the target was already gone, the cerebral pilots slipping nineteen hulls through the gaps between energy lances as if the whole fleet shared one nervous system. The Eden's five Titan Cannons spoke in a rolling sequence Halcyon timed to the millisecond — strip a shield with the first, punch the wound with the second, and a seventy-thousand-ton Obsidian warship folded in on itself before its self-repair could so much as begin. They killed three in the opening pass. They had never killed even one, before today.

For those eleven minutes, the bridge let itself believe.

Then the enemy stopped studying them.

Greenwich felt the change before the sensors named it — the way the Obsidian movements went from curious to deliberate, the way the two flagships at the heart of the armada finally deigned to turn. The restraint that had let Harris land his ram, the patient indifference that had treated the human fleet as a specimen rather than a threat, simply switched off. And the math the Eden had been beating reasserted itself, all at once.

The GFC Tempest died first, bow to stern, a lance through her reactor before Halcyon could pull her clear. Greenwich felt the pilot flinch — felt all of them flinch, the shared mind recoiling from the place where a brother ship had been a heartbeat before.

"Stay in the work," he said quietly, for them and for himself. "Stay in the work, Halcyon."

"I'm in it, Admiral." Her voice had thinned. Holding nineteen ships had become holding eighteen, then sixteen, the awareness contracting around each loss like a wound closing over.

He gave the order he had been saving. "Launch the corvettes. Now, while we still have their eyes on us."

This had been the plan beneath the plan — the reason Greenwich had brought corvettes to a battle they could not win. Six of them dropped from the Eden's flanks, killed their engines after a single calculated push, and went utterly dark: no power, no signature, nothing for an enemy sensor to find but cold drifting metal. Each one carried a single cerebral pilot, and each pilot flew her corvette on momentum and mind alone, threading the silent hulls down toward the orbital Titan cannon platforms that ringed Trident — the great planetary guns the Dra'Kor Vhel'Tan had left dark in their first sabotage. If the Eden's fleet was the fist the enemy watched, the corvettes were the knife no one was meant to see.

"Keep their attention on me," Greenwich told the bridge. "Every gun we have. Make us the loudest thing in the sky."

They did. The Eden drove straight into the throat of the armada, Titan Cannons hammering, and for a while the sheer audacity of it worked — a flagship that should have run was instead charging, and the Obsidians turned to meet the insult. Greenwich spent his remaining ships like a man counting out coins he knew he'd never need again. The Resolute went covering the Eden's flank. The Ardent went buying ninety seconds. Each death narrowed Halcyon's voice further, and Greenwich came to understand, listening to her, that the cerebral pilots did not simply lose a ship when a ship was lost. They lost a piece of the body they were wearing. They felt every one of them go.

"Admiral." It was Halcyon again, and something in her had changed — gone calm in the particular way of people who have done their arithmetic and found the answer they expected. "The corvettes are through. Three platforms are answering. The orbital cannons are coming back online."

Greenwich let out a breath he'd been holding since the Vindicator died. Three. Not the dozen the planet deserved, but three great guns waking over Trident that the enemy had thought safely murdered — three that would still be there when the Eden was not, pointed down at the war for the surface, waiting for the ground to need them.

It was enough. It would have to be.

"Send the firing authorities to Gladius," he said. "Captain Orion's people will want them. And flag the platforms as friendly-locked — I don't want the enemy turning our own guns on the city the second we're gone."

"Already done, Admiral." Halcyon's eyes opened then, for the first time since the engagement began, and found his across the bridge. There were only a handful of ships left in the awareness she held now, and they all knew it. "They've stopped trying to disable us."

"I know."

"They're closing to finish it."

"I know that too." Greenwich finally lit the cigar. The good Cuban, the one he'd carried through thirty years for a day he'd always known would come and had never quite believed in. The smoke curled up into the filtered air, and the bridge crew watched him do it, and took from it exactly what he meant them to: that their Admiral was not afraid, and that they need not be either. "Halcyon. You and yours have done more today than the Coalition will ever know to thank you for. I'm going to give you one more order and then I'm going to stop giving them."

"Sir."

"Take everything left. The reactors, the maneuvering reserve, all of it. Pour it into the cannons and the pilots. We hold this spot in the sky as long as there's a spot to hold — every minute we cost them up here is a minute the ground gets to run." He looked around the bridge, at the officers and the pilots and the cradles, at the last flagship of a fleet that no longer existed. "We are not the shield anymore, people. We're the clock. So let's give the planet every second we've got."

The Eden's reactors screamed past every limit they'd been built to respect. The Titan Cannons fired and fired, and the cerebral pilots wrung impossible turns out of a ship far too vast for them, and for a few minutes more the pride of STARCOM Fleet stood alone against an armada and would not move.

Then a beam wide enough to swallow the bridge whole reached out of the dark.

Greenwich saw it coming. He had time, in the way men sometimes do, for one last clear thought, and it was not of the fleet or the war or even of Harris. It was the small absurd hope that the three guns would be enough, that somewhere down on the burning surface a soldier he would never meet would live because the sky had stayed loud a little longer.

"Halcyon," he said. "Let go of the others. Just hold this one. Hold us."

"I've got us, Admiral." And in the cradles, the cerebral pilots — who had felt every ship in the fleet die against the inside of their own skulls — drew their shattered awareness in until it held only the Eden, only each other, only the man with the cigar telling them they had done enough.

The beam struck.

At GFC Base Gladius, in a war room two thousand meters of rock away from the sky, a technician would report a moment later that Admiral Greenwich's communications had gone completely dark. There was no distress call. There was no final transmission. There was the Eden, holding its place in the sky against everything the Obsidians could bring — and then there was not, and the orbit over Trident belonged wholly to the enemy.

But three great guns still turned in the dark above the planet, awake and friendly-locked and waiting, where a few hours before there had been none. And that was the last word Admiral Greenwich ever sent to the surface, though no one down there would understand for some time that he had sent it, or what it had cost to write.
