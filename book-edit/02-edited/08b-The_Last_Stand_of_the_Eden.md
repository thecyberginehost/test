Chapter 9: The Last Stand of the Eden
Date: December 16, 2888
Time: 0728 Galactic Standard Time (GTC)
Location: Flagship – GFC Eden, high orbit over Trident

*(Provisional number — runs concurrent with the Liberation boarding; see DISCREPANCY-REPORT A3/G13 for renumbering.)*

The Vindicator did not explode so much as cease.

Admiral Greenwich had seen ships die. He had seen them gut themselves on mines, crack along the keel, bloom into the slow silent fire that was the only kind space allowed. He had never seen one simply stop existing. The enemy beam had been wider than the warship it erased — a flat sheet of pale light that opened without a sound, held for less than a second, and took the Vindicator and the two GFC cruisers flanking her out of the universe in a single stroke. No wreckage worth the name. No bodies. Just the after-image burning on the back of Greenwich's eyes and the place in formation where his oldest friend had been.

Harris had rammed a dying ship into an enemy flagship to earn that death. The Obsidians had answered him the way a man answers a mosquito.

"Admiral." His flag captain's voice was very careful. Renna Okonkwo had served under him for nine years and Greenwich could hear, under the discipline, that she was as close to the edge as the rest of them. "Orders."

The bridge was watching him. The bridge was always watching him, and in the next ten seconds it would decide whether it was a fighting crew or a frightened one, and it would decide based on his face. So Greenwich took the cigar from his breast pocket — the good Cuban, thirty years old, the one he'd promised himself he would only ever smoke on the day he knew he wasn't coming home — and he set it unlit between his teeth, and he let them all see him do it.

"We're going in," he said.

Nobody had ever fought these things and lived to write the manual. That was the part that sat coldest in Greenwich's chest as the Eden came about. Everything the Coalition knew about the enemy could be written on the back of a ration card: that they came out of nowhere, that their hulls knit themselves back together faster than you could hole them, that their armor shrugged off a capital round, and that somewhere in that armada were beams that unmade ships whole. His crews were not veterans of this war. There were no veterans of this war. There were only the men and women who happened to be alive at the start of it, and the smoking absence where the *Vindicator* had taught the first lesson.

"Set condition one through the fleet," Greenwich said. "Every hull, every gun. And get me the pilots."

The Eden answered like something half-alive, because she very nearly was.

She did not fly the way other warships flew. On any other vessel in the Coalition the pilots sat sealed two decks down in a Neural Bridge, their will relayed up through fiber and faith. Not here. Here they lay in a half-ring of reclined cradles at the back of Greenwich's own bridge, eight men and women who never touched a control in their lives, and the *Eden* was not a ship they flew so much as a body they wore. They were Mindreavers — the rarest gift the Coalition had, and the most quietly feared, GRID-found and fleet-bonded and worth more than the warship that carried them. When they worked, four hundred meters of steel moved like a held breath.

"We're here, Admiral." Halcyon's voice came from the lead cradle, and Greenwich was grateful, as he always was, that she sounded like a person and not like the machine she was steering. She was a small woman from one of the agricultural worlds, prone to humming old harvest songs when she thought the bridge couldn't hear, and right now her voice was tight with the same fear as everyone else's and she was using it anyway. "I've got the whole fleet. All nineteen. They're scared, sir. So am I. We're still here."

"Good. Stay scared. Scared people pay attention." Greenwich almost smiled. "You have the fleet, Halcyon. I want us moving like one fist, not nineteen fingers. The second you see one of those wide beams charge, you get my ships out of its mouth — I don't care what it costs us in position. We do not feed them another Vindicator."

"Understood, sir." A pause, and her voice dropped so that only the forward stations caught it. "I felt it. When the *Vindicator* went. All of us did — like a light going out in the next room." Then, steadier: "It won't happen to us the same way. I won't let it be that easy for them."

She could not have known how much she was promising.

Greenwich turned to Okonkwo before the fleet committed. "Before we're in the thick of it — the Praetorians. Are they away?"

"Both teams, sir. Shadowhawk and Ironclad burned for the Liberation the moment we cleared gravspace." Okonkwo's jaw tightened. "Lieutenant Barkley's last check-in had them inbound to the hull. They mean to clear the station and hold it, or scuttle it if they can't."

"God go with them." Greenwich meant it. The Liberation was the planet's nerve center, every automated gun and comms relay routed through her, and he had sent two of the finest fireteams in the Coalition into her corridors knowing the odds and saying none of it aloud. "Keep a channel open for Barkley, no matter what's happening out here. If that man calls, I want to hear him over my own guns."

"Aye, Admiral."

He set his hands on the rail, and the cigar between his teeth, and watched the armada fill the viewport — seventy warships and ten of the great slab-sided transports and the two flagships at the dark heart of it, and around them a haze of fighter-craft thick as flung sand. Eighty-two hulls. Against nineteen and the Eden.

"All ships," Greenwich said, and the fleet channel carried it to every bridge that still had a crew. "This is Greenwich. You all saw what they did to Harris. I'm not going to stand here and tell you we're going to win this, because you're not children and I'm not a liar. But every minute we keep these bastards looking up here is a minute they're not looking *down there* — at the cities, at the evac lanes, at the people we came to cover. So we are going to be the loudest, meanest, most expensive thing these things have ever had the misfortune to point a gun at. Make them earn the sky. All ships — advance. Titan Cannons free."

The Eden surged, and her fleet went with her, and for eleven minutes the Coalition got to believe.

The armada had been built to kill ships that flew like ships — that lagged, that telegraphed, that carried a half-second of human reflex stitched into every turn. The Eden's flotilla did not have that half-second. Where an enemy gunner led a target, the target was already elsewhere; Halcyon and her pilots slipped nineteen hulls through the lattice of incoming fire as though the whole fleet shared one spine, one set of nerves, one animal certainty about where death was going to be and how not to be standing in it. When the first wide beam charged — that telltale swelling glare Greenwich would have nightmares about for the rest of his short life — Halcyon had four ships clear of its throat before it fired, and it burned empty space, and the bridge let out a single ragged cheer.

The Eden's five Titan Cannons spoke in a rolling sequence the pilots timed to the millisecond: strip a shield with the first round, drive the second into the wound before the enemy hull could knit, and a seventy-thousand-ton Obsidian warship folded in on itself and died. They killed three in the opening pass. They had never killed even one.

"That's it!" someone shouted from gunnery — young Petrov, who would not see noon. "That's how you do it! Hit 'em twice in the same hole!"

"Belay the celebrating, keep the timing," Okonkwo snapped, but there was fire in her now, the good kind, and across the bridge spines straightened and hands steadied. For eleven minutes the pride of STARCOM Fleet danced in the teeth of an armada and made it bleed, and Greenwich let himself feel, for the first time since the *Vindicator* died, something that wasn't grief.

Then the enemy stopped losing.

It was not that they brought up more guns. It was that they began to *learn*. Greenwich felt the shift before the sensors found a name for it — the way the Obsidian fire stopped chasing his ships and started waiting for them. The first sign was the *Tempest*. Halcyon threw her into the kind of slip-turn that had saved a dozen hulls already, and this time the enemy salvo was simply *there*, blooming in the empty space she was turning into, as if the gunners had stopped aiming at where the *Tempest* was and started aiming at where a clever pilot would send her next. The cruiser took the salvo amidships and broke in half, bow to stern, and Greenwich felt the loss land on the bridge a half-second before the sensors reported it, because in the lead cradle Halcyon cried out as though she'd been struck.

"They moved into it," she said, and there was something new in her voice, something close to panic. "I had her clear — I *had* her — and they put it where I was going to be. They're not aiming at us anymore, Admiral. They're aiming at *me*. At how I think."

"Then think different," Greenwich said, gentler than the words. "Don't give them a pattern to catch."

"They catch it anyway." Her breath came short. "Every trick, the second time I use it, it's already dead. It's like fighting something that's read the next page before I turn it."

And that was the horror of them, the thing none of the manuals had — the thing Greenwich would not live to put in a report. The Obsidians adapted. Whatever advantage you showed them, you showed them only once; the second time it was a grave you walked into. The Eden's flotilla had one edge in all the universe, the impossible unity of minds steering steel — and the enemy was learning the shape of that edge in real time and folding their entire armada around it, methodically, patiently, the way a hand closes around a struggling bird. There was a cold to it that had nothing to do with the void. Halcyon kept flinching from something in the dark she couldn't name, a vast pressure leaning back against her mind whenever she reached out to hold her ships — *as if the enemy had hands of the same kind, and more of them, and stronger.* She didn't have the words for it. She only knew that every time she got clever, something on the other side got there first.

They started dying in earnest then.

The *Ardent* went buying ninety seconds, standing into a beam meant for the Eden so that her sister could slip the noose; Greenwich watched her captain hold the line on an open channel and say only, "Tell my wife I held," before the light took him. The *Resolute* died covering the corvette launch. The *Lance* and the *Valkyrie* died together, tangled in the same desperate knot of fighters, their gunners firing to the last. And every single time, Greenwich heard it twice — once on the sensors, and once, half a heartbeat earlier, in the cradles behind him, where the pilots felt each ship go out like a finger severed from their own shared hand. By the sixth loss Halcyon had stopped crying out. She had gone somewhere quiet and terrible, and she flew the dwindling fleet from inside it, and Greenwich understood that the cerebral pilots were not losing ships. They were losing pieces of a single body they had all agreed to wear, and they were choosing, again and again, not to let go.

"Halcyon." He kept his voice level. "Can you still give me the corvettes?"

"I can give you the corvettes." Flat. Spent. Certain. "Just tell me it's worth them."

"It's worth everything."

This was the plan beneath the plan — the reason a man who knew he was outnumbered four-to-one had still brought corvettes to the fight. Six of them dropped from the Eden's flanks in the chaos of the dying, fired their maneuvering thrusters exactly once, and then went dark: no engines, no power, no signature, nothing for an enemy sensor to find but six pieces of cold drifting scrap among ten thousand others. Each carried a single pilot, one of Halcyon's own, and each of those pilots flew her corvette on momentum and mind alone, falling silent and unseen toward the orbital Titan cannon platforms that ringed Trident — the great planetary guns the enemy's saboteurs had switched off in the war's first hours and never thought of again. If the Eden's fleet was the fist the armada watched, the corvettes were the knife held low, in the dark, where no one was looking.

"Keep their eyes on me," Greenwich told the bridge. "I want this ship to be the most insulting thing in the sky. Drive us at the nearest flagship. Make them want us dead more than they want anything else in this system."

Okonkwo looked at him. "Sir, that puts us inside the envelope of both —"

"I know exactly where it puts us, Captain."

She held his eyes for a moment, and then she nodded, because she did too. "Aye, Admiral. Taking us in."

The Eden drove into the throat of the armada with all five Titan Cannons hammering, and the audacity of it worked the way audacity sometimes does against an enemy that thinks in patterns: a flagship that should have been running was instead charging, alone, into a death it had clearly chosen, and the Obsidians turned the weight of their attention to meet the affront. It bought the corvettes their silence. It cost the Eden everything else. Her shields — the proudest in the fleet, thrice the strength of any other Coalition hull, the gift of Greenwich's old GRID connections — began to fail in sheets, and the great ship shuddered under hits that would have ended any other vessel three times over.

It was then, with the deck bucking under his boots, that the channel Greenwich had ordered kept open finally spoke.

"Eden, this is Shadowhawk Six." Barkley's voice came thin through the interference, ragged with close combat, the sound of energy weapons behind every word. "We can't hold her, Admiral. We can't even reach the control room — they've got it sealed and stacked. The Liberation's lost. Requesting authorization to scuttle. We put a Dread Wing nuke into her and we deny these things every gun and every secret she's carrying. I say again — request authorization to scuttle the Liberation, over."

The bridge had gone still around the transmission. Greenwich closed his eyes for exactly as long as he could afford, which was not long.

The Liberation was the planet's heart. Scuttling her meant blinding Trident's own defenses, finishing with a Coalition warhead what the enemy had started. It meant the men who couldn't reach the pods in time. It meant admitting, out loud, on an open channel, that the thing they had come to save could not be saved.

"Authorized," Greenwich said. "Shadowhawk Six, you are authorized to scuttle. Get your people off that station — you've got five minutes and not one second I can promise you past it. And Barkley —" He stopped. There were a hundred things and no time for any of them. "None better. You hear me? None better. Get who you can to the surface. The fight's going there now."

"Aye, Admiral." A breath. "Holding the line. Shadowhawk out."

The channel closed, and somewhere above the burning planet two fireteams began to run for their pods, and Greenwich turned back to the only fight he had left to give.

"Halcyon. The corvettes."

"Almost." Her voice had thinned to a thread. The Eden was down to six ships now, then five, the bridge lights stuttering with each hit, smoke curling from a ruptured panel where the *Eden*'s own systems were beginning to give. "They're through the screen. Three platforms answering — the cannons are cold, but they're answering, sir. Bringing them up. It's slow. Everything's slow when I'm — when there's this much —"

"Take your time," Greenwich lied. "Take all the time in the world."

And the cerebral pilots of the GFC Eden, who had felt nineteen ships die one by one against the inside of their own skulls, reached down through six silent corvettes to the dead guns ringing a dying world, and one by one, they woke them up.

"Three," Halcyon breathed. "Three platforms, online and locked friendly. They're— Admiral, they're *ours*. The enemy can't turn them. I made sure. Three orbital Titan cannons, live over Trident, and they don't even know it yet."

Three. Not the dozen the planet deserved. But three great guns waking in the dark above Trident, three the enemy had counted as dead and would learn otherwise the hard way, three that would still be turning over the burning cities long after the Eden was scattered atoms — pointed down, friendly-locked, waiting for the ground to need them.

It was enough. It would have to be.

"Send the firing authorities to Gladius," Greenwich said. "Flag the platforms friendly-locked and burn the override keys behind them — I will not have our own guns turned on the city the moment we're gone. Make sure Orion's people know what they've been given." He drew a slow breath. "Then we're done taking orders from anybody, and we're done giving them. Halcyon — let the others go. Just hold this one. Just hold the Eden."

In the cradles, the surviving pilots drew their shattered, fraying awareness in from the dark — released the last of the dying fleet, the corvettes, the great cold guns, all of it — until there was only the Eden, and each other, and the man at the rail with the unlit cigar, telling them they had done enough.

"We've got her, Admiral," Halcyon said, and for the first time in an hour she sounded almost at peace. "We've got her to the end."

Greenwich lit the cigar.

The smoke curled up into the filtered air, and the bridge crew watched him do it, and took from it exactly what he meant them to: that their Admiral was not afraid, so neither would they be. The deck heaved. The shields were gone. Okonkwo stood at her station with blood running from a gash above her eye and called firing solutions in a voice that did not shake, and Petrov's replacement worked the Titan Cannons until the barrels glowed, and the Eden held her place in the sky and would not move, pouring fire into an enemy that no longer needed to hurry.

"You all did more today than the Coalition will ever know to thank you for," Greenwich said, to the bridge, to the cradles, to the ship. "Every second we steal up here, somebody breathes down there. So let's steal them a few more."

He saw the last beam begin to charge.

He had time — the way men sometimes do, at the very end — for one clear thought, and it surprised him by being a small one. Not the fleet. Not Harris. Not the thirty years or the medals or the great burning machine of the war. Just the simple, stubborn, irrational hope that the three guns would be enough. That somewhere on the broken surface a soldier he would never meet, in a fight he would never see, would live one minute longer because the sky had stayed loud — because an old man and eight frightened pilots and a ship full of brave fools had decided that the price of that minute was worth paying in full.

"Halcyon," he said. "Take us into it. Right down its throat. If they want the Eden, make them choke on her."

"Yes, sir." No fear in it now. Just the harvest-world girl, humming something old and low under her breath as she turned four hundred meters of dying warship to face the light.

At GFC Base Gladius, two thousand meters of rock beneath the burning sky, a technician would look up from his board a moment later and report, in a voice gone suddenly flat, that Admiral Greenwich's communications had cut out completely. There was no distress call. There was no final transmission. There was the Eden, holding her place against everything the armada could bring — and then there was not, and the high orbit over Trident belonged wholly to the enemy.

But three great guns still turned in the dark above the planet — awake, and friendly-locked, and waiting — where an hour before there had been none. And that was the last word Admiral Greenwich ever sent to the surface, though it would be some time before anyone down there understood that he had sent it, or what it had cost to write.
