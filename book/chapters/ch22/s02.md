There was a salvage run that afternoon, a clinic two blocks east that the cleared edge had not reached yet, and Aaron said he was going.

Priya looked at him a long beat. He could not see the look, only feel its shape in the silence. Then she said, "Okay," which was not okay, and got his boots.

He had done this kind of thing alone for thirty-one years and most of one apocalypse. He told himself he could walk two blocks. The yard ended in a lip of broken curb he did not remember, and his foot came down on nothing, and Marcus's good hand closed around his arm before he could fall. Not a grab. A catch, easy, like a man steadying a door. Aaron's whole body went rigid against it.

"I've got it," he said.

"I know," Marcus said, and did not let go.

They went out through the gap in the wall. Priya walked at his left and talked the ground for him the entire way, low and even, the same voice she used on the dying. Curb in three steps. Glass, going left around it. There's rebar at your knee, lift. He had spent the climb reading the world a layer deeper than anyone, calling the wave before it broke, naming the gap in a thing's guts and driving a kill into it. Now a sixteen-year-old's grandmother voice in a nurse's mouth was his sight. He hated how much he needed it. He took every word.

A floor had buckled across the street, a slab heaved up into a ramp of rubble, and there was no clean way over it. Aaron stopped at the foot of it, blind, fifty points of body, and felt the old refusal rise in him like a stat he could not edit. I work alone. He had said it to Priya in a stairwell. He had said it to Hutch every time the man tried to thank him. He had built the whole war out of it. It had been a lie even then. It was just a louder lie now.

"I can't," he said, and the words cost him more than any edit had. "I can't do this part."

"No," Priya agreed. Not unkind. Just true.

Marcus came around to his bad side and Priya to his good one, and between them they took his weight onto the slab. He felt his feet leave sure ground and trusted two other people to find it for him. His hands wanted to grab, to manage, to do. Priya held one of them flat against the rubble so he could feel the pitch of it himself, and that was the thing that broke him a little, that she gave him back the piece he could still have. They went up the slab the three of them, slow, his arms over their shoulders, Marcus grunting once when the strapped arm took a pull it should not have.

"Sorry," Aaron said.

"Shut up," Marcus said, with no heat in it at all.

At the top of the slab the wind moved across his face and he could not see what was up there, the street, the light, any of it. He could feel the two bodies holding him level. He thought about the math he could no longer read, the enormous unaccountable sum of being kept alive by people he had spent every breath insisting he did not need. The numbers were gone. The thing the numbers had been measuring was right here, one on each side of him, breathing hard.

They should have been three. The thought came plain and would not soften. Tess would have gone up this slab first, light on it, called the loose stone before anyone trusted weight to it, turned at the top and waited with that grin for him to catch up slow. She did not get to carry him. She did not get to be carried. She got a loop of rebar in a yard and a name on a board. Hutch should have had his shoulder under Aaron's other arm and would have, gladly, after everything, after the gap Aaron left open that killed him. Two of them did the work of four because two was what was left, and they did it without a word about who was missing, because some grief you carry by carrying each other.

Aaron let himself be carried. That was the whole of it. The man who worked alone let two people he had chosen, and who had chosen him back, hold him up over ground he could not see, and he stopped fighting their hands.

"Down on the far side," Priya said. "Slow. We've got you."

"I know," Aaron said, and this time he meant it.

They brought him down off the slab into the street, the three of them moving as one weight, and went on toward the clinic in the long afternoon light he could only feel.
