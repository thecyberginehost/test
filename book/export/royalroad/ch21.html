He went past the boss. Past the patched payout and the patched heal and the patched aggro, all of it his, all of it expected. The win condition of the whole settlement, the flag that decided when the measurement was satisfied, and it sat there legible to his account, exactly as he had left it. Read. Never edited. No patch had ever closed it because no one had ever made the move. It could not patch a move that had never been played.

It would land for that reason and no other. Not because the god was sloppy. It had read him the whole descent and patched everything he had ever done. The one code it could not pre-empt was the code it had thought was past him, and he had learned to read it. Every nosebleed, every level burned, every floor that hurt going down had bought this. Not a hole left open. Literacy, finally caught up to the floor of the world.

And it bent for him. Only for him. A settlement-bound account could not touch its own win condition, the rule held shut against every other hand in the city. His did not read shut. His read open, the rules folding aside for the one account flagged exempt and not telling him why.

He set the cursor on the value.

Ninety-nine behind his eye. One keystroke past it.

His hand did not shake. The boss came around. Marcus held the half-step. The grammar lay open under his cursor, the whole heart of the thing waiting on a single flip, and he chose it, knowing exactly what crossing a hundred took from him, and began.

He flipped it.

One value. Not the boss, not the payout, not any of the sixteen old tricks the thing had eaten. The flag at the floor of the place, the number the trial checked to ask who had won. CORE_GRAMMAR's win condition. He moved it from the god's column to theirs.

Narrow, no wider than the Suffocation cap had been. All its weight in where it sat.

A settlement-bound account could not have done it. The rule held shut against every hand in the city. His read it open. The terms folded aside for the one flag marked exempt, and did not say why. He did not ask. No time, with a thing that had never answered.

The bill came as one act.

The progress charge hit first, the forge again, the drop from buying the crew their door. Fifty levels asked. He had thirteen, twelve above the floor, and the account paid them all the way down, Level 13 to Level 1. HP guttered from a wall to a sliver. Perception collapsed to ten, the eyes he was born with. Wits to ten. The thirty-eight it could not collect, the trial carried, an account it would not parse.

Then the tax.

He had one point of room. Ninety-nine, a rung to a hundred, and the Tier-3 tax was twenty. It came up past that one point and kept coming, more than the room he had, nowhere for the rest to go but the wall. The ceiling took it. It stopped flush, full. Decode_debt, one hundred out of one hundred, closed.

And the layer began to go.

He had known it would. That was the whole of the choice, made a breath ago with his hand steady. The gray comment-text he had read through every floor of the descent dimmed now, the way a screen dims when the charge bleeds out, except it would not come back on. The gray went out. Not torn, not strobing, not smeared and recovered as on the bad floors. It was not there. Behind his right eye the pressure he had hated for nineteen days let go, and the relief was the worst thing he had ever felt, because it meant the door had shut on the room he lived in. For one heartbeat he felt the shape of a question he would never read the answer to. Whether the debt was weight he chose to carry, or a leash left on the one account it could not open, set to blind itself so it stayed unpatched. Then the layer that could answer was off, and the question went with it.

He kept everything, in the place where reading used to be. The Suffocation cap, the skill, the door at the heart of the dungeon. Every edit he had ever written still held, locked, none taking the win-flag back. Only the eyes that wrote it were gone.

Aaron opened his actual eyes wider, because they were all he had now.

The dungeon came at him flat. No grammar under the floor, no comment-text bleeding up the boss's edge. Just stone and dust and blood that was Marcus's and Priya's and his own. To his left a man on one knee behind a shield gone crooked and heavy in his grip. Beyond it a woman gray to the lips, nothing left to give. The boss above them, his own work in his own face, coming around for the seventeenth time.

He could not read it. The world was the size of his eyes again, and it was loud, and it was close, and under the floor a flag he could no longer see had turned over and begun to count for their side.

The boss did not know yet.

It came around for its seventeenth pass the same as the sixteen before, his own work in its face, sure of the floor it stood on. The flag under that floor had turned. It could not feel the turn. Neither could Aaron, not anymore. It changed mid-stride.

The sound came first. Not a roar. A wrongness in the air, low, the pitch of a room when the power cuts and every fan you stopped hearing stops at once. The boss's weight broke step. One of its limbs, the reaching one it had taken from the door he annotated on the second day, came down on stone that should have held it and did not, and the limb went through, and the thing pitched.

Aaron looked. He could not pry the seam, could not read what the trial wrote into its status as the win-flag counted against it now instead of for it. The grammar of its ending ran somewhere under the floor where he would never see grammar again. He got the outside of it, the part anyone in the room got, and it was enormous.

The boss came apart from the inside out.

Light bled from the joints first, the cold seam-light it had carried since the apex opened, and then the light reversed and ran inward and took the shape with it. A god had built this thing out of his ledger, every trick he ever ran stitched into a single body to enforce the trial against them. Now the trial had nothing left to enforce. The grief of it surprised him. He had braced for the dark. He had not braced for needing to be told.

The standing weight that had pressed on the room for the whole fight lifted off them. The air changed. Even blind he felt it change.

Quiet came down after it like a second collapse.

No one cheered. Priya was crying without sound, holding him and holding herself up with the same grip. Marcus had gone down to one knee behind a shield his torn arm could no longer keep square, and stayed there, breathing.

Aaron sat in the dark of his own head with the dust settling on him and listened to the room not trying to kill them. He could not see that the threat was over. He believed it because two people he had spent the climb pretending he did not need had told him so, and that was enough.

The apex went quiet like a building after the last fan dies.

It happened in layers he could only hear and feel. The boss's weight had stopped its fight with the floor, and into the space where its noise had been came the smaller sounds the fight had drowned all day. Water somewhere, dropping. The tick of cooling stone. Marcus's breathing, ragged, three feet to his left. Aaron sat in his own dark and let it all reach him one piece at a time, because one piece at a time was the only way anything reached him now.

"The light's going," Priya said. She said it slow, like she was making sure of it before she let herself believe it. "Aaron. The rift-light. It's draining out of the walls. It's just going."

He remembered the apex when it opened, cold seam-light bleeding from every joint of the place, the whole district-sized throat of it lit like the inside of a wound that would not close. Daylight. The deepest floor in the world, and somewhere above it the sky still ran.

Aaron's body chose that moment to understand what it had been through. The shaking started in his hands and went up his arms and would not stop. A Level 1 body, fifty hit points, ten of everything that used to be thirty. The cold of the stone came up through him with nothing to hold it off. He had spent a fortune to be this small, and sat blind on the cold stone and let it shake him.

He had started on a Tuesday. He let himself land on that, because the contrast was the only thing big enough to hold the rest. An office, a dashboard that swore a dead server was healthy while he lost the argument about it. A man who read what systems hid and got soft-deflected for his trouble. And here. The apex. The deepest, deadliest place the world had grown, the measurement built to settle a bet placed on the whole species, draining quiet around three people who had walked in on purpose and walked out alive. The ground under him would have erased the man from that Tuesday in a breath. It would have erased the crew that fled that first stairwell. It did not. Three of them stood up.

The fourth was a floor he could not read in time, the surge-gate not far above this one, where his blurred sight came a half-beat late. Tess should have called this clear. She read terrain the way he read code, and she would have named every cold seam in this throat going dark, and she was not here to do it. The win sat in his chest next to the hole where she went. Aaron nodded into the dark. He could not see the place go safe. He could only feel the cold and the shaking and the quiet, and trust the two voices telling him the worst thing the world had ever built had stopped being a threat. He had spent the whole climb insisting he did the trusting alone.

He sat blind at the bottom of the world and let them tell him it was over.

For a long time after, nothing came.

That was the new shape of things. He had spent the whole climb pulling the layer open with his own two hands, prying where it was sealed, paying the tab to read one more line. Now there was no line to read and no hands to read it with. He sat in the dark and the dark stayed dark, and he understood that this was what blind meant. Not that the world went quiet. That the asking went one way only, and the answer never came back.

So when the answer came, he felt it before he understood it.

It did not surface. Nothing surfaced anymore, and he was past expecting it to. It arrived.
